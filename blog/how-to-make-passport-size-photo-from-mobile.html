What You Should Know Before Using This Tool

Before you pull out your phone and start clicking, there are some critical details that will determine whether your passport photo gets accepted or rejected. Understanding these upfront will save you from the frustration of multiple retakes and failed uploads.

First, every country has different passport photo specifications. India requires 35x45 mm with a white background. The USA wants 2x2 inches (51x51 mm) with a white or off-white background. The UK uses 35x45 mm but allows a light gray background. Canada specifies 50x70 mm. If you submit a photo with the wrong dimensions or background color for your specific country, it will be rejected regardless of how good the image looks. Always check the official requirements for your country before starting.

Second, lighting is more important than your camera quality. A 48-megapixel phone camera in poor lighting will produce a worse passport photo than a 12-megapixel camera in good natural light. Passport photos require even, shadow-free illumination on your face. Harsh shadows, red-eye from flash, or uneven lighting are automatic rejection reasons. The best setup is standing facing a window during daytime with the light falling directly on your face.

Third, the background must be plain and uniform. Patterns, textures, doors, windows, plants, or even shadows on the wall behind you can cause rejection. The background should be a solid color — white for India, white or off-white for most other countries. If you don't have a perfectly plain wall, you can hang a white bedsheet behind you. Online background removal tools can help, but starting with a clean background makes the process much easier and produces better results.

Fourth, your expression and posture matter. Most countries require a neutral expression — no smiling, mouth closed, eyes open, and looking directly at the camera. Your head should be centered and occupy 70-80% of the photo height. Tilting your head, wearing glasses with reflection, or having hair covering your eyes can all lead to rejection. Fifth, file size and format requirements are strict. Most online passport portals accept only JPG format. The file size is usually capped between 10KB and 300KB. A high-resolution phone photo straight from the camera can be 3-5 MB, which is way too large. You will need to compress the image without changing its pixel dimensions. Our Compress Image to 50KB Online Free tool is designed exactly for this purpose. If you want to understand the mechanics behind compression, read Lossy vs Lossless Compression Explained.

Sixth, print quality depends on DPI. For physical printouts, your image needs to be 300 DPI. This means a 35x45 mm Indian passport photo should be exactly 413x531 pixels. A 2x2 inch US passport photo should be 600x600 pixels. If the pixel count is wrong, the photo will print at the wrong size or look blurry. Always verify both dimensions and DPI before submitting or printing.

Step-by-Step Guide to Make Passport Size Photo from Mobile

This guide is divided into two parts: Part 1 covers taking the perfect photo with your phone. Part 2 covers converting it to official passport specifications using free online tools.

Part 1: Taking the Perfect Passport Photo with Your Phone

1

Prepare Your Background

Find a plain white or off-white wall. If your wall has texture, patterns, or colors, hang a white bedsheet or chart paper behind you. Make sure the background is evenly lit with no shadows. The background should extend at least 1 foot above your head and to both sides. Remove any objects, pictures, or switches visible in the frame.

2

Set Up Lighting

Stand facing a large window during daytime. Natural daylight is the best and most flattering light for passport photos. Avoid direct sunlight that creates harsh shadows. If natural light is not available, use two light sources — one on each side of your face at equal distance — to eliminate shadows. Never use the phone's built-in flash; it causes red-eye, shiny skin, and harsh shadows.

3

Dress Appropriately

Wear dark-colored clothing that contrasts with the white background. Avoid white, cream, or very light colors that blend into the background. Do not wear uniforms, camouflage, or clothing that looks like official government attire. Remove sunglasses, hats, and large hair accessories. Religious head coverings are allowed in most countries but must not cast shadows on your face.

4

Position Yourself Correctly

Stand 4-6 feet away from the camera. The photographer (or a friend/family member) should hold the phone at your eye level. Do not take a selfie — the arm's length distance is too close, and the angle is slightly downward, which distorts facial proportions. Your head and shoulders should be visible, with your head centered in the frame. Look directly at the camera lens, not the screen.

5

Use the Rear Camera, Not the Front

Always use your phone's rear (main) camera for passport photos. Rear cameras have higher resolution, better color accuracy, and less distortion than front-facing selfie cameras. The front camera on most phones is designed for video calls, not high-quality prints. If you are alone, use your phone's timer feature (3 or 10 seconds) so you can position yourself before the photo is taken.

6

Maintain Neutral Expression

Keep your mouth closed and maintain a neutral expression. Do not smile, frown, or raise your eyebrows. Your eyes must be fully open and visible. Look directly into the camera lens. Relax your face — a tense expression looks unnatural. Take multiple shots so you can choose the best one.

7

Review and Select the Best Shot

Check your photos immediately. Look for: even lighting on both sides of the face, no shadows behind you, eyes fully open, no hair across the face, no red-eye, and a neutral expression. The best photo should show your full face clearly with sharp focus on the eyes. If any of these are off, adjust and retake.

Common Mistakes & Why They Happen

Mistake 1: Taking a Selfie Instead of Using the Rear Camera

Many people take passport photos as selfies because they are alone. Selfies create several problems: the angle is slightly downward, which makes the forehead look larger and the chin smaller. The arm's length distance is too close, causing facial distortion. The front camera has lower resolution and worse dynamic range than the rear camera.

Why it happens: People don't realize how much camera distance and angle affect facial proportions. A passport photo needs to look exactly like you, without distortion. Always ask someone to take the photo from 4-6 feet away using the rear camera. If alone, use a tripod or prop your phone against books and use the timer feature.

Mistake 2: Using Flash in a Dark Room

Someone takes a passport photo at night using the phone's flash because they need it urgently. The result is red-eye, shiny skin, harsh shadows behind the head, and an overall unprofessional look.

Why it happens: Flash photography is fundamentally unsuitable for passport photos. The light is too harsh, too directional, and too close to the lens. Always use natural daylight or evenly distributed artificial light. If you must shoot at night, use two lamps on either side at equal distance, and place a white sheet behind you. Never use direct flash.

Mistake 3: Wearing White or Light-Colored Clothes

A person wears a white shirt against a white background. When the photo is processed, their shoulders and neck blend into the background, creating a "floating head" effect. The portal rejects it for poor contrast.

Why it happens: Passport guidelines require clear separation between the subject and background. Dark-colored clothing (navy blue, black, maroon, dark green) creates strong contrast against white backgrounds. Avoid white, cream, beige, or any color similar to your background. This is one of the easiest mistakes to prevent.

Mistake 4: Cropping the Photo Manually Without Knowing Exact Dimensions

Someone opens their phone's built-in editor, crops the photo "to look about right," and uploads it. The portal rejects it because the aspect ratio is wrong, the head is too small, or the pixel dimensions don't match requirements.

Why it happens: Manual cropping is guesswork. Passport specifications are precise to the millimeter. A difference of 5 pixels can cause rejection. Always use a dedicated passport photo tool that applies the exact template for your country. If you need to resize to specific pixel dimensions manually, our Resize Image to 200x230 Pixels Online Free tool can help you understand exact pixel-based cropping.

Mistake 5: Uploading PNG or HEIC Files to Government Portals

A user takes a photo on an iPhone, which saves as HEIC by default. They upload this directly to a government portal. The portal shows an error: "Invalid file format." Or they save as PNG thinking "PNG is higher quality," but the portal only accepts JPG.

Why it happens: Almost all Indian and international government portals accept only JPG format. HEIC is an Apple-specific format that most non-Apple systems cannot read. PNG files are unnecessarily large for photographs. Always convert your final passport photo to JPG before uploading. If you are unsure about format differences, our JPG vs PNG: Which Format Should You Use? guide explains when to use each format.

Mistake 6: Not Checking File Size Before Upload

A user creates a perfect passport photo but the file is 1.5 MB. The portal limit is 300KB. They upload anyway and get rejected. They compress the image using a random app that also reduces pixel dimensions, making the photo too small.

Why it happens: Compression and resizing are two different operations. You need to compress (reduce file size) without resizing (changing pixel dimensions). Use a tool that specifically compresses to a target file size while preserving exact pixel dimensions. Q2: What are the exact dimensions for an Indian passport photo?

Indian passport photos require 35x45 mm dimensions at 300 DPI, which equals 413x531 pixels. The head should occupy 70-80% of the photo height (25-35 mm). The background must be plain white. File size should be between 10KB and 300KB. Eyes must be open, mouth closed, and expression neutral. Both ears should be visible.

Q7: Can I smile in my passport photo?

Passport photo expression rules vary by country. India requires a completely neutral expression — no smiling, mouth closed, eyes open. Most countries strongly discourage or prohibit glasses in passport photos. The USA does not allow glasses unless medically necessary with a signed doctor's note. India prefers no glasses.
